Lead Essay

The Typewriter as Editorial Instrument

By Eleanor Voss
New York  —  April 2026

There is a persistent myth that monospace type belongs to the programmer, to the terminal, to the cold blue light of a server room. This essay argues otherwise. The monospace letter is, above all else, a precise thing — and precision has always been the first virtue of great editorial design.

When designers in the 1960s reached for the IBM Selectric, they were not making a concession. They were choosing a tool that imposed discipline: no kerning to agonize over, no optical corrections to negotiate, no typographer's ego lurking in the spacing. Just the word, the line, the page.

The Democratic Glyph

Fixed-width type carries an implicit argument about equality. The i and the m share a column width. The period and the ampersand take up identical real estate. This is not a limitation — it is a commitment to a certain flatness of form, one that forces meaning to come from the words themselves rather than the nuanced shape of the letterforms.

Contemporary literary publications — among them certain Scandinavian quarterlies and downtown New York pamphlets — have rediscovered this quality. Their pages feel unhurried. The columns breathe. The monospace font, paradoxically, creates more white space by making the reader aware of the invisible grid underneath every line.
