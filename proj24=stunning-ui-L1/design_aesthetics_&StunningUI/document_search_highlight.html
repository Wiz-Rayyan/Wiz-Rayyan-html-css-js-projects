The Design of Everyday Things

Don Norman  ·  Chapter 1: The Psychopathology of Everyday Things

Good design is actually a lot harder to notice than poor design, in part because good designs fit our needs so well that the design is invisible, serving us without drawing attention to itself. Bad design, on the other hand, screams out its inadequacies, making itself very noticeable.

Discoverability and understanding

Two of the most important characteristics of good design are discoverability and understanding. Discoverability asks: is it possible to even figure out what actions are possible and where and how to perform them? Understanding asks: what does it all mean? How is the product supposed to be used? What do all the different controls and settings mean?

With complex devices, discoverability and understanding are more difficult. When I turn on a modern television set, I'm often baffled by the choices. Each manufacturer has a different way of arranging menus and controls. Good design makes discoverability trivial and understanding immediate.

Affordances

An affordance is a relationship between the properties of an object and the capabilities of the agent that determine just how the object could possibly be used. A chair affords support and, therefore, affords sitting. Most chairs also afford lifting — they are light enough that most people can lift them. Heavy chairs don't afford lifting; they do afford sitting.

The term affordance was invented by the perceptual psychologist J. J. Gibson in his 1979 book, The Ecological Approach to Visual Perception. Gibson wanted to emphasize that perception is more than just what the eye sees — it is what the combined effect of the environment and the organism produces.

Signifiers

Affordances determine what actions are possible. Signifiers communicate where the action should take place. We need both. Affordances represent the possibilities in the world for how an agent can interact with something. Some affordances are perceivable, others are not. Signifiers must be perceivable, otherwise they serve no purpose.

The push plate on a door is a signifier — a signal to where to push and that pushing is the appropriate action. Without signifiers, users are left to guess and often guess wrong. Good design makes signifiers clear, prominent, and unambiguous.

Feedback and conceptual models

Feedback communicates the results of an action. Without feedback, people are left to guess whether an action was successful. Feedback must be immediate: even a delay of a tenth of a second is noticeable and distracting. Feedback must also be informative. Poor feedback can be worse than no feedback at all, because misleading feedback will cause people to create incorrect conceptual models of the design.

A good conceptual model allows us to predict the effects of our actions. Without a good model, we operate by rote, following instructions without understanding what will happen if we deviate from those instructions. A mental model is the explanation of someone's thought process about how something works in the real world.

Human-centered design

Human-centered design is a philosophy, not a precise set of methods. It requires observing people in their natural activities, generating ideas, building prototypes, testing them with real users, and iterating. The goal of human-centered design is to produce a product that someone finds useful, understandable, and enjoyable.

Design is concerned with how things work, how they are controlled, and the nature of the interaction between people and technology. When done well, the results are brilliant and wonderful products. When done poorly, the results are miserable products that make our everyday life more difficult and frustrating.
