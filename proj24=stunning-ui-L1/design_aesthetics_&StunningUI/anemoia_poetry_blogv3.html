The Light Through Someone Else's Window

"I have mourned places I have never been, and wept for afternoons that belonged to strangers."

There is a particular grief in the antique store — not for the chipped porcelain or the foxed photographs, but for the warmth you can feel radiating off them, a warmth that was never yours to begin with. You press your thumb to the corner of someone's holiday snapshot and feel the sun of a July you were never born into.

The word for this is anemoia. The Dictionary of Obscure Sorrows gave it to us, and we have been carrying it gratefully ever since, the way one carries a photograph of a grandmother one never met.

The old house, before

A Sunday, unnamed

End of summer, always

On the Feeling

Vespers for the Unlived Summer

You know it by its specific texture: a Kodachrome slide held up to light, the saturated orange of some stranger's picnic, a woman laughing with her head thrown back, a car that no longer exists on a road that has since become a parking lot. You were not there. You could not have been there. And yet.

Anemoia is not sadness exactly. It is something more like homesickness for a country you invented in your sleep — warm, unhurried, always late afternoon, always just about to sit down to a meal someone's grandmother has been cooking all day.

"You grieve a version of ordinary life that was never yours — and that, perhaps, never existed at all."

A Fragment in Verse

Coda

I dreamed myself into a kitchen
that smelled of rendered fat and radio static,
a summer I could not have been alive for —
the linoleum, the fan, the long particular light
of five o'clock in a year I wasn't born.

I woke with the grief of it still on my hands.
