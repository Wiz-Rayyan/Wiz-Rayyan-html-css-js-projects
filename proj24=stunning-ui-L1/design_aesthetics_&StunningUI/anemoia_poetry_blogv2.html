On Longing for Summers
That Were Never Mine

By Elspeth Morrow  ·  Illustrated by R. Hartwell

There are afternoons I remember with terrible precision — a porch I never sat on, a radio crackling standards through a window that was never mine to open. The screen door snaps behind a woman whose face I cannot see, and I feel something seize in me: grief for a life that was not lost, because it was never had.

"We mourn not the past we lived, but the past we suspect we were owed."

Philosophers have no good word for it. Psychologists call it a trick of media, a confusion of borrowed imagery for personal memory. Perhaps. And yet the longing does not feel borrowed. It feels inherited — as though some sadness skipped a generation and arrived, confused, in me.

I have driven through towns where the gas stations still have hand-painted prices, where the diners have not changed their signage since my parents were young, and felt not nostalgia but something stranger: recognition of a place I have never been.
