Linguistic analysis
Root: ص–م–د (ṣ-m-d) — lexical garden
Classical lexicographers give al-Ṣamad two primary senses: (1) the one to whom all beings direct their needs, and (2) the absolutely solid — having no hollow, no cavity, no dependence. Rāzī enumerates twelve attributes implied by the term.

What does al-Ṣamad exclude?
Claim: al-Ṣamad denies all dependence
∴ God has no needs — no food, no offspring
Obj: Does "hollow" (ajwaf) refer to body?
Res: Rāzī: it negates potentiality, not body per se
Claim: al-Ṣamad denies all dependence
P1: A being with needs requires another for fulfillment
P2: Dependence implies temporal origination (ḥudūth)
∴ God is necessarily independent in all respects
Obj (Philosophers): Does this conflict with divine love/will?
Res: Love/will in God are identical with His essence — no external object required
Obj (Mujassima): Does "solid" imply corporeal density?
Res: Solidity here = ontological completeness, not physical density
Comparative tafsīr — al-Ṣamad
Rāzī: Systematically enumerates twelve meanings of al-Ṣamad from lexicography and theology, arguing they all converge on absolute self-sufficiency and ontological completeness — the ground of all dependence.

ص-م-د appears only in Sūrat al-Ikhlāṣ (112:2) — making it one of the rarest roots in the Qurʾān. Rāzī notes this uniqueness as itself theologically significant: no other context qualifies the term, so all meaning must be inferred from this single verse.
Corner medallion — Sufi allusion
The mystical key — al-Ṣamad and fanāʾ

While Rāzī approaches al-Ṣamad through rational theology, he acknowledges the Sufi reading: if God alone is the Besought-of-all, then the path of the mystic is to annihilate all other objects of desire — arriving at fanāʾ (annihilation in the divine) and baqāʾ (subsistence through God).

Al-Junayd (d. 910): "The Ṣamad is the one in whom all things end and from whom all things begin — the mystic's journey is to become a vessel through which al-Ṣamad acts."

The corner medallion in Rāzī's manuscript tradition encodes this allusion — the rotating geometric pattern (shamsa) represents the divine essence around which all created beings orbit, each petal a different created attribute, the center empty of all but God.
