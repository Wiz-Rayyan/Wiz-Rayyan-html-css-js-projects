featured verse

The Light I Never Knew

On the peculiar ache of loving a world you were never part of

by the editors

There is a window in a house that no longer stands, and through it pours a light I have never seen but know precisely. It is the gold of four o'clock in a summer that belonged to someone else — to my grandmother's grandmother, to a woman who pressed flowers into a diary I will never read.


This is the peculiar cruelty of anemoia: to miss something that was never yours to lose. To grieve a life you only glimpsed in photographs, in the curve of a handwritten address, in the sound a door makes in an old film.

On Trains & Departures

What the platform photographs could not show us

correspondence, undated

Every photograph of a train station contains a goodbye. I have collected seventeen such images — strangers' farewells, caught in silver and stopped forever — and in each one I feel the steam, the wool coat, the particular way time moved when it moved slowly.


To study the past is to become fluent in a language no one speaks. You learn it perfectly, useless and beautiful, and then you mourn that there is no one left to answer.

You can be homesick for a place you have never been — the body does not require logic, only longing.

Palimpsest
I have worn a dress from 1943
and stood in the kitchen of a stranger
and heard the radio say something small and true
and known, with a certainty that has no right to exist,
that I have been here before —
not in body, not in dream,
but in some country that lives
between remembering and wish.
— from the contributors
